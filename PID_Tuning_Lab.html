Clues to look for

  • Kp ↑: faster rise, more overshoot, smaller (but not zero) error.
  • Ki ↑: error removed faster, more overshoot and slower oscillation.
  • Kd ↑: less overshoot, more damping, big spike in u(t).
  • Look at the final value first: only integral action takes the error to zero.

Servo rig

A DC motor turns an arm through a 32:1 gearbox. An optical encoder on the motor shaft measures the angle. A microcontroller runs the PID loop and drives the motor through an H-bridge from a 12 V supply. Change a gain and the rig replays the move.

What to watch

  • Kp only: the arm stops short of the dashed setpoint (orange wedge = error) or swings past it.
  • Add Kd: the D bar in the microcontroller brakes the arm as it approaches, so it stops swinging.
  • Add Ki: the I value grows slowly and pushes the arm the last few degrees onto the setpoint.
  • Add load with P only: the arm sags and stays down. With Ki it climbs back.
  • ±12 V limit: big gains ask for more voltage than the supply has; PWM sticks at 100 % and the move is slower than the plot without the limit.
  • Encoder noise with large Kd: the PWM duty and motor voltage chatter.

The PID controller

The controller works on the error e(t) = r(t) − y(t):

u(t) = Kp·e(t) + Ki·∫e(t)dt + Kd·de(t)/dt          C(s) = Kp + Ki/s + Kd·N·s/(s + N)

The derivative is filtered (N is the filter bandwidth in rad/s) because a pure derivative would amplify noise without limit. Every real PID controller does this.

  • Proportional, Kp: reacts to the present error. More Kp gives a faster, more accurate response but less damping. On its own it leaves a steady-state error unless the plant contains an integrator.
  • Integral, Ki (lag compensator): adds up past error, so it keeps pushing until the error is zero. Too much Ki over-integrates: overshoot, slow oscillation and finally instability.
  • Derivative, Kd (lead compensator): reacts to how fast the error changes, which adds damping and reduces overshoot. Too much Kd amplifies noise and produces large spikes in the control signal.

Tuning procedure (follow it in order)

  1. Proportional test (Rule 1). Set Ki = Kd = 0 and increase Kp in steps. If the output overshoots above the demand and the error falls, the system is a lead system and needs derivative action. If the error hardly changes and the output stays below the demand, it is a lag system and needs integral action. If you see both, it is lead-lag and needs both.
  2. Choose Kp (Rule 3): the value that gives a reasonable response without a large overshoot.
  3. Add derivative in small steps to damp the oscillation, keeping Ki = 0.
  4. Add integral in small steps to remove the steady-state error. Stop before the overshoot comes back (Rule 4).
  5. Check the result with a disturbance and with sensor noise, and look at the control signal: a real actuator cannot deliver huge spikes.

Useful ideas

  • Pole location tells the story. Poles further left decay faster; poles further from the real axis oscillate faster; any pole on the right means instability. Watch the pole map while you drag a slider.
  • Derivative kick. With the derivative acting on the error, a step in the reference makes a huge spike in u(t). Switching to derivative on measurement removes it without changing the poles.
  • Integrator windup. If the actuator saturates, the integral keeps growing and causes big overshoot. Anti-windup (clamping) stops the integrator while the output is saturated.
  • Ziegler–Nichols. Find the ultimate gain Ku (P-only gain at which the loop just oscillates) and the period Tu, then use Kp = 0.6Ku, Ki = 1.2Ku/Tu, Kd = 0.075KuTu. It gives an aggressive starting point, not a final answer.
  • Pole-zero cancellation. For the lab plant 4/(s + 2)², the gains Kp = 8, Ki = 8, Kd = 2 make the controller zeros sit on the plant poles, so the loop becomes 8/s and the response is first order with no overshoot.
